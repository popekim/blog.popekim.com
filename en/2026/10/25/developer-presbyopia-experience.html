A Developer's Guide to Living with Presbyopia

Pope Kim Oct 25, 2026
  • aging
  • presbyopia
  • glasses
  • health
  • dev

If you want to keep working as a developer for a long time, taking care of your body is pretty important. Sure, it's mostly brain work, but you still need a body that can keep up. So I've always made an effort to stay reasonably healthy. That doesn't mean hitting the gym and working out like crazy every day. For me, it's mostly about eating well, sleeping well, and avoiding unnecessary strain on my body.

When something does start hurting, though, I get a little more proactive. Years ago, when I started having shoulder pain, I switched to a tenkeyless keyboard. Without the number pad, I could keep my mouse closer to my body. Eventually, I got rid of the conventional mouse altogether and switched to a roller mouse.

When my back started giving me trouble, I spent some time doing intensive strength training. I also got a standing desk and eventually invested in a Herman Miller chair. That's generally how I've dealt with these things. When something goes wrong, I try to figure out what's causing it and change something, whether it's exercise, equipment, or daily habits.

But no matter how well you take care of yourself, some things are unavoidable as you get older. One of them is presbyopia, the age-related loss of the ability to focus on nearby objects.

Presbyopia Creeps Up on You

Presbyopia doesn't suddenly appear overnight. Your eyes gradually lose their ability to focus at close distances, and most people start noticing the inconvenience sometime in their 40s.

I've always been nearsighted, so I've worn glasses for most of my life. Until my mid-40s, I had no real trouble getting by with my regular glasses.

That doesn't mean nothing was changing. Small text on my phone was becoming harder to read. I gradually started holding my phone a little farther away, and at some point I began increasing the font size.

But that was about it. My computer monitors were still perfectly comfortable to use, and reading books wasn't much of a problem either. So I didn't think much of it.

The real trouble started when I got a new pair of glasses.

New Glasses, Worse Experience at the Computer

In my mid-40s, I had my eyes checked and got a new pair of glasses for my nearsightedness. My old prescription was no longer quite right, so naturally I figured getting it corrected was a good thing.

Then I sat down at my computer with the new glasses, and something felt off.

It wasn't that I couldn't read the text on the monitor. I could. It just wasn't comfortable anymore. I found myself squinting or straining my eyes to read things that I used to look at without even thinking.

Things in the distance were sharper, but the computer monitor I stared at all day had become uncomfortable. I had paid for new glasses, only to make my work more difficult. Great.

Later, I learned that this could be related to presbyopia. Glasses for nearsightedness are primarily meant to correct distance vision, while the ability to focus on nearby objects decreases with age. A stronger prescription for distance vision can actually make close-up work less comfortable.

Apparently, my old glasses had been better suited for looking at my monitors.

I Was Afraid of Progressives, So I Bought Multiple Pairs of Glasses

I'd heard that progressive lenses were the usual solution for presbyopia. But I'd also heard stories from people who couldn't get used to them.

Some said they made them dizzy. Others complained about distortion, and some eventually gave up wearing them altogether. After hearing all that, I wasn't particularly interested in spending a bunch of money on progressive lenses that I might not even be able to use.

So instead, I bought several pairs of glasses.

One for distance, one for computer work, and another for reading. Each pair used single-vision lenses with a prescription suited to its intended purpose.

And honestly, this worked pretty well.

When I was at my computer, I'd wear the glasses made for the distance between my eyes and the monitors. When I wanted to read, I'd switch to the reading glasses, which were designed for a shorter distance.

I was especially happy with the computer glasses. I could see the entire monitor comfortably without constantly adjusting my head position or trying to look through a particular part of the lenses.

The problem was going outside.

Sometimes I'd need to look at something in the distance and then check my phone. Or I'd be at a restaurant trying to read the menu. Obviously, I couldn't keep switching glasses every time I needed to look at something closer. Carrying multiple pairs everywhere wasn't much fun either.

The setup worked great when I was sitting in one place doing one particular task. For everyday life, though, it wasn't particularly convenient.

Eventually, I Tried Progressive Lenses

So I finally decided to give progressive lenses a try.

I was a little worried. After hearing so many people complain about dizziness and difficulty adjusting, I figured I might have the same problem.

But when I actually tried them, they were surprisingly fine.

Of course, progressive lenses have their limitations. The upper portion is designed for distance vision, while the lower portion handles closer distances. There's also distortion toward the edges. Some people need time to get used to moving their eyes and head differently.

But at least for me, the adjustment wasn't particularly difficult. I guess everyone is different.

By the way, I don't think you necessarily need to spend a fortune on progressive lenses. I got mine at a discount optical chain while visiting South Korea, using relatively inexpensive Korean-made lenses. They were surprisingly cheap compared to what I'd pay in Canada, and I've been perfectly happy with them. Maybe more expensive lenses have their advantages, but I don't have any complaints about mine, so I don't see much reason to switch.

Once I started wearing progressives, everyday life became noticeably easier. Looking at something far away, checking my phone, reading small labels in a store, or looking at a restaurant menu could all be done with one pair of glasses.

That didn't mean I threw away all my single-vision glasses, though.

In fact, that's when I finally started figuring out the best way to use all of them.

The Best Solution for Me Was a Combination of Progressives and Single-Vision Glasses

These days, I wear progressive glasses pretty much all the time. Even at home. Unless I have a specific reason to switch, I just keep my progressives on.

But when I'm going to spend a long time doing something specific, like working at my computer or reading a book, I switch to single-vision glasses designed for that task.

I keep computer glasses where I work and reading glasses where I usually read. Basically, I have several inexpensive pairs of glasses scattered around the house.

I can certainly use my computer while wearing progressive lenses. But for extended work, single-vision lenses are much more comfortable. With progressives, the areas that provide clear vision at different distances are limited, so you may have to adjust your head position to look at the monitor comfortably.

With single-vision computer glasses, the entire lens is optimized for the distance to my monitor. That gives me a much wider area of comfortable vision. When you spend most of the day working at a computer, it makes quite a difference.

The same goes for reading. Glasses specifically made for reading distance are simply more comfortable for long reading sessions.

Of course, if I plan to read for a long time or work on a computer while I'm out, I'll bring the appropriate glasses with me. But for quickly checking my phone or reading a menu, my progressives are more than enough.

This combination has made my life a lot more comfortable.

I started out using only multiple pairs of single-vision glasses, eventually added progressives, and finally settled on using both. It probably took me a year or two to figure out what worked best for me.

It would've been nice to know all this from the beginning, but I suppose some things you just have to figure out through trial and error.

Even Reading Glasses Need Upgrades

So once you've adjusted, that's the end of it, right?

Unfortunately, no.

Presbyopia continues to progress for a while. Glasses that worked perfectly well a few years ago may gradually become less comfortable. Small text that used to be easy to read starts looking blurry again.

Based on my experience, it seems reasonable to have the prescription checked every three years or so and get stronger reading glasses if necessary.

Of course, presbyopia doesn't progress at the same rate forever. The changes are generally more noticeable during your 40s and 50s, and things tend to stabilize around your 60s. So I'm not suggesting that everyone will need stronger glasses every three years for the rest of their lives.

One nice thing about having multiple pairs of glasses is that there's a way to save some money here.

Let's say your reading glasses are no longer strong enough to comfortably read a book. That doesn't necessarily mean you need to throw them away. They might still work perfectly well for a computer monitor, which is typically farther away than a book.

So you can repurpose your old reading glasses as computer glasses and just buy a new pair for reading. Obviously, you'd want to make sure the prescription is actually suitable for your monitor distance.

Reusing glasses this way can save some money. Since I already keep multiple pairs around the house, there's no reason to replace every single one whenever my prescription changes.

The Blurriness That Glasses Can't Fix

There's one problem that hasn't completely gone away, even after figuring out how to live with presbyopia.

My eyes sometimes just feel blurry or hazy.

Whether I'm wearing progressives or reading glasses, there are moments when my vision simply doesn't feel as clear or comfortable as it used to. Even when I'm looking at something at the correct focal distance, things can feel a little off.

It's especially noticeable in dim lighting.

When I was a kid, my mother would sometimes ask why I was reading in such a dark room and tell me to turn on the lights. Back then, I never understood what she was talking about. I could see perfectly fine.

Well, I think I understand now. LOL.

Lighting that used to be perfectly adequate for reading now feels too dim. I need brighter lights, and small text or low-contrast text is noticeably harder to read.

I don't know whether all of this is caused by presbyopia. Aging can also bring problems like dry eyes and other changes in vision, so I wouldn't automatically blame everything on presbyopia. If things stay blurry even with the right glasses, getting an eye exam would probably be a good idea.

In any case, getting the right glasses doesn't magically give you your younger eyes back.

I guess this is what getting old feels like.

Fortunately, Coding Is Still No Problem

The good news is that I still have no trouble working at my computer.

With my dedicated single-vision computer glasses, I can stare at my monitors for hours without much discomfort. At least when it comes to development work, presbyopia isn't really getting in my way.

I'm still using 24-inch monitors, and my Windows display scaling is still set to 100%. I haven't felt the need to increase font sizes or buy larger monitors.

Of course, that might change in a few years.

If it does, I figure I'll just increase the Windows display scaling to 125% or 150%. If the text becomes too small to read comfortably, I'll make it bigger. Isn't that one of the nice things about computers?

And if I eventually need larger monitors, I can get those too. There are plenty of ways to adjust a computer setup.

Getting presbyopia doesn't necessarily mean your productivity as a developer has to suffer. At least that hasn't been my experience so far.

You Can't Stop Aging, but You Can Adapt

Looking back, the way I've dealt with presbyopia isn't all that different from how I've handled other physical problems over the years.

When my shoulders hurt, I changed my keyboard and mouse. When my back hurt, I worked on strengthening my muscles and changed my desk and chair. When my eyes started giving me trouble, I got several pairs of glasses and changed how I used them.

There is one difference, though.

Shoulder or back pain can sometimes improve with exercise or changes in daily habits. Presbyopia, on the other hand, isn't something you can reverse by working out harder. At some point, you just have to accept it and adapt.

I can't say I'm particularly happy about getting older. It's annoying when things you used to do without a second thought gradually become inconvenient. Life was definitely simpler when one pair of glasses was all I needed.

But what can you do?

Get the glasses you need, adjust your working environment, and figure out a solution whenever something new becomes inconvenient. I'm sure more physical changes are coming as I get older, and I'll probably deal with those the same way.

I suppose the point of taking care of your body isn't to stay young forever. It's to keep yourself capable of doing the things you enjoy for as long as possible.

For now, all I need to do is switch glasses when necessary, and I can still code just fine. Good enough for me.
